Robotics theory notes, geometric tools, and implementation examples.

This page brings together conceptual notes and practical examples on robot kinematics, screw theory, Lie groups, Lie algebras, Jacobians, and mechanism modeling. The aim is to provide a concise but rigorous entry point for students, researchers, and practitioners in robotics.

What this page covers

A compact theory-to-practice guide for modern robotics

A professional understanding of robotics begins with kinematics: the study of motion without explicitly considering forces. From there, deeper geometric tools such as screw theory, Lie groups, and Lie algebras provide elegant representations for rigid-body motion, velocity, and configuration.

These concepts are central in serial manipulators, parallel robots, mobile robots, and mechanism analysis, and they also serve as a bridge to dynamics, control, planning, and optimization.

Part I · Foundations

Foundations of robot kinematics

Serial mechanisms Parallel mechanisms Mobility analysis Workspace

Kinematics studies the geometry of motion. In robotics, it answers questions such as: How does a set of joint variables determine the position and orientation of an end-effector? How many independent motions does a mechanism possess? Where do singularities occur? What is the reachable workspace?

Forward kinematics

Maps joint variables to task-space configuration:

\[ x = f(q) \]

Given \(q\), compute position and orientation \(x\).

Inverse kinematics

Finds joint values corresponding to a desired pose:

\[ q = f^{-1}(x) \]

This problem may admit multiple, unique, or no solutions.

Mobility / Degree of freedom

For many mechanisms, mobility is estimated by:

\[ M = \lambda (n-j-1) + \sum_{i=1}^{j} f_i \]

where \(n\) is number of links, \(j\) is number of joints, and \(f_i\) is the DOF of the \(i\)-th joint.

Key idea: Kinematics is the language that connects mechanism structure, joint coordinates, workspace, and motion capability. It forms the first mathematical layer in understanding any robot architecture.

Part II · Rigid-body motion

Rigid-body motion, coordinates, and homogeneous transforms

A rigid body in 3D space is described by both orientation and position. The orientation is typically represented by a rotation matrix \(R \in SO(3)\), while the translation is represented by a vector \(p \in \mathbb{R}^3\). Together, these form the homogeneous transformation matrix:

\[ T = \begin{bmatrix} R & p \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \in SE(3) \]

This representation is fundamental because it allows composition of motions, coordinate changes, and frame transformations to be written in a compact matrix form. If frame \( \{B\} \) is expressed relative to frame \( \{A\} \), then \( ^A T_B \) maps coordinates from \( \{B\} \) to \( \{A\} \).

\[ {}^{A}p = {}^{A}R_{B}\, {}^{B}p + {}^{A}p_{B} \]

For serial manipulators, forward kinematics is typically obtained by chaining transforms:

\[ T_{0n}(q) = T_{01}(q_1) T_{12}(q_2)\cdots T_{(n-1)n}(q_n) \]
Configuration space: the set of all possible robot configurations, typically parameterized by joint variables.
Task space: the space in which the robot’s end-effector position and orientation are specified.
Workspace: the set of end-effector poses that the robot can physically reach.
Posture: a specific arrangement of the internal joints corresponding to a given pose.
Why homogeneous coordinates?

They unify rotation and translation into a single object, which makes geometric reasoning, coordinate transformation, and implementation much cleaner.

Part III · Geometric mechanics

Screw theory for robotics

Screw theory provides a unified language for representing rigid-body motion and force. Rather than separating translation and rotation as unrelated quantities, screw theory describes them as parts of a single geometric object. This makes it especially powerful in mechanism analysis, Jacobian derivation, singularity analysis, and kinetostatic modeling.

Twists

A twist describes instantaneous rigid-body velocity. It combines angular and linear velocity into a single 6-dimensional vector:

\[ \xi = \begin{bmatrix} \omega \\ v \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6 \]

Here, \( \omega \in \mathbb{R}^3 \) is the angular velocity component, and \( v \in \mathbb{R}^3 \) is the linear component. For a screw axis passing through a point \(q\) with pitch \(h\), the linear component is:

\[ v = -\omega \times q + h \omega \]

Special cases:

  • Pure rotation: \( h = 0 \)
  • Pure translation: \( \omega = 0 \)
  • General screw motion: simultaneous rotation and translation along a line

Wrenches

A wrench describes forces and moments acting on a rigid body:

\[ \mathcal{F} = \begin{bmatrix} n \\ f \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6 \]

where \(f\) is the force vector and \(n\) is the moment about a reference point.

Exponential coordinates of motion

If a rigid body moves along a constant screw axis \(\xi\) by scalar amount \(\theta\), its transformation can be written as:

\[ T(\theta) = \exp(\hat{\xi}\theta) \]

This formula is central in the product-of-exponentials representation of robot kinematics.

Key insight: screw theory unifies rotational and translational motion just as it unifies force and moment. In robotics, this creates a natural geometric bridge between mechanism structure, motion, and statics.

Part IV · Geometry and algebra

Lie groups and Lie algebras in robotics

Lie groups and Lie algebras offer a mathematically rigorous way to represent rigid-body motion, differential motion, and frame transformations. In robotics, the most important groups are \(SO(3)\) for rotations and \(SE(3)\) for full rigid-body motions.

\(SO(3)\): rotation group

\[ SO(3)=\left\{R \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}\mid R^TR=I,\ \det(R)=1 \right\} \]

Represents orientations in 3D. A rotation matrix is orthonormal and has determinant 1.

\(SE(3)\): rigid motion group

\[ SE(3)=\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} R & p \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \,\middle|\, R\in SO(3),\ p\in\mathbb{R}^3 \right\} \]

Represents full pose: orientation and position.

Lie algebras

\[ \hat{\omega}= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -\omega_3 & \omega_2\\ \omega_3 & 0 & -\omega_1\\ -\omega_2 & \omega_1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \in so(3) \]
\[ \hat{\xi}= \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\omega} & v\\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \in se(3) \]

Exponential map

The exponential map connects Lie algebras (infinitesimal motion) to Lie groups (finite motion):

\[ R = \exp(\hat{\omega}\theta), \qquad T = \exp(\hat{\xi}\theta) \]

In practice, this means a robot motion generated by an axis and motion magnitude can be converted into a rigid-body transformation.

Adjoint transformation

Twists and wrenches change coordinates between frames using the adjoint operator:

\[ \mathrm{Ad}_{T} = \begin{bmatrix} R & 0 \\ \hat{p}R & R \end{bmatrix} \]
\[ V_s = \mathrm{Ad}_{T}V_b \]

Why this matters in robotics

  • Provides coordinate-free and compact motion descriptions
  • Supports rigorous kinematic and dynamic derivations
  • Enables product-of-exponentials robot modeling
  • Improves numerical consistency compared with ad hoc parameterizations
  • Connects geometry, optimization, control, and estimation
Interpretation:

Lie groups represent configurations on a manifold; Lie algebras represent tangent motions at the identity. The exponential map connects the two.

Part V · Differential kinematics

Jacobians, velocity analysis, and singularities

The Jacobian maps joint rates to end-effector velocity and is one of the most important objects in robotics. For a manipulator with joint coordinates \(q\), the velocity relation is:

\[ V = J(q)\dot{q} \]

Jacobian meaning

The columns of the Jacobian represent the contribution of each joint to the end-effector’s instantaneous motion.

Singularity

A singular configuration occurs when the Jacobian loses rank. In such cases, motion or force transmission becomes deficient or ill-conditioned.

Applications

Jacobians are used in inverse velocity kinematics, manipulability analysis, stiffness studies, control, and trajectory generation.

Static duality

Joint torques are related to wrench by:

\[ \tau = J^T \mathcal{F} \]

Manipulability idea

A common indicator is based on:

\[ w = \sqrt{\det(JJ^T)} \]

Practical note

Near singularity, numerical inverse kinematics often requires regularization or damped least-squares methods.

\[ \dot{q} = J^T(JJ^T+\lambda^2I)^{-1}V \]

Part VI · Implementation

Robotics in Python: five-bar mechanism example

The five-bar mechanism is a useful planar closed-chain example for teaching forward and inverse kinematics. It is commonly used to demonstrate nonlinear constraints, multiple working modes, and coupled joint behavior.

A minimal class structure for teaching forward and inverse kinematics can be organized as follows: 

import numpy as np
from math import cos, sin, atan2, sqrt

class FiveBar:
    """
    A simple educational class for a planar five-bar mechanism.
    Link naming can be adapted to the geometry you use in your notes.
    """

    def __init__(self, l1, l2, l3, l4, base):
        self.l1 = l1
        self.l2 = l2
        self.l3 = l3
        self.l4 = l4
        self.base = base

    def circle_intersection(self, c1, r1, c2, r2):
        x1, y1 = c1
        x2, y2 = c2
        d = np.hypot(x2 - x1, y2 - y1)

        if d > r1 + r2 or d < abs(r1 - r2):
            return None

        a = (r1**2 - r2**2 + d**2) / (2*d)
        h = sqrt(max(r1**2 - a**2, 0.0))

        xm = x1 + a * (x2 - x1) / d
        ym = y1 + a * (y2 - y1) / d

        xs1 = xm + h * (y2 - y1) / d
        ys1 = ym - h * (x2 - x1) / d

        xs2 = xm - h * (y2 - y1) / d
        ys2 = ym + h * (x2 - x1) / d

        return (xs1, ys1), (xs2, ys2)

    def forward(self, theta1, theta2):
        """
        Compute end-effector candidates from actuated angles.
        """
        A = np.array([0.0, 0.0])
        B = np.array([self.base, 0.0])

        P = A + self.l1 * np.array([cos(theta1), sin(theta1)])
        Q = B + self.l4 * np.array([cos(theta2), sin(theta2)])

        candidates = self.circle_intersection(P, self.l2, Q, self.l3)
        return candidates

    def inverse(self, x, y):
        """
        Compute actuator angles for a desired point (x, y).
        """
        theta1 = atan2(y, x)
        theta2 = atan2(y, x - self.base)
        return theta1, theta2

# Example usage
robot = FiveBar(0.2, 0.3, 0.3, 0.2, 0.4)
points = robot.forward(np.deg2rad(40), np.deg2rad(120))
print(points)
Teaching note: A five-bar mechanism is excellent for explaining constraint equations, branch selection, assembly modes, and the difference between joint space and Cartesian space.

Part VII · Numerical computing

Robotics in MATLAB

MATLAB remains a powerful environment for rapid prototyping, symbolic derivation, matrix computation, and visualization in robotics. It is particularly useful for teaching transforms, Jacobians, workspace plots, and trajectory generation.

Typical MATLAB topics

  • Denavit–Hartenberg parameterization
  • Symbolic forward kinematics
  • Jacobian derivation
  • Workspace visualization
  • Inverse kinematics solvers
  • Trajectory interpolation

Example transform computation

% Homogeneous transform in MATLAB
syms theta d a alpha real

T = [cos(theta), -sin(theta)*cos(alpha),  sin(theta)*sin(alpha), a*cos(theta);
     sin(theta),  cos(theta)*cos(alpha), -cos(theta)*sin(alpha), a*sin(theta);
     0,           sin(alpha),             cos(alpha),            d;
     0,           0,                      0,                     1];

disp(simplify(T));
Recommendation: MATLAB is ideal for symbolic derivations and quick numerical tests, while Python offers flexibility for larger software pipelines, simulation, and integration with modern ML tools.

Closing note

Summary

Robotics kinematics begins with links, joints, and coordinate frames, but its most elegant and powerful formulations come from geometry. Screw theory unifies rotation and translation, twists and wrenches; Lie groups and Lie algebras provide the natural structure for rigid-body motion; and Jacobians connect configuration to velocity, force, singularity, and control.

These tools are not only mathematically elegant—they are practical. They form the backbone of manipulator modeling, parallel robot analysis, estimation, optimization, motion planning, and robot control.

In one sentence: kinematics tells us what motion is possible, screw theory tells us how motion and force are represented geometrically, and Lie theory tells us how rigid-body motion is structured mathematically.
