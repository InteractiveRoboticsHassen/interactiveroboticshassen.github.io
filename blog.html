Research Blog

From mathematical robotics to physics-informed intelligence and quantum robotics.

This blog is organized around three connected research directions. The first develops the mathematical and analytical foundations of robotics through mechanism theory, screw theory, Lie groups, Lie algebras, differential geometry, kinematics, singularity theory, stiffness, and geometric mechanics. The second explores hybrid intelligence: combining first-principles physics with neural networks to produce robot models that are data-efficient, interpretable and physically consistent. The third investigates quantum computing for robotics, including quantum machine learning, quantum optimization, reinforcement learning, motion planning and high-dimensional robot configuration search.

Category 1

Robotics — Mathematical & Analytical Approaches

Robots are physical machines, but their motion is fundamentally geometric. A mathematical description should explain not only where the end-effector moves, but why a mechanism has a particular mobility, what motions its limbs permit, what forces its structure constrains, where singularities occur, and how architectures can be synthesized systematically.

R

Product of exponentials

\[ T(q)= e^{[\mathcal S_1]q_1} e^{[\mathcal S_2]q_2} \cdots e^{[\mathcal S_n]q_n} M \]

A coordinate-efficient alternative to purely frame-by-frame parameterization.

Differential motion

\[ V_s = J_s(q)\dot q \]

The Jacobian columns are the transformed joint screws expressed in a common frame.

Screw reciprocity

\[ \mathcal W^T \mathcal T = 0 \]

Reciprocal screw systems connect permitted motion with constraint forces.

Category 2

Physics-Informed Neural Networks — The Hybrid Approach

Classical robotics begins with equations. Modern machine learning begins with data. A powerful middle ground is to make the neural network respect the physics we already know while allowing it to learn the parts that are difficult to model accurately. This creates a hybrid model: analytical where knowledge is reliable, learned where uncertainty remains.

π

Robot dynamics

\[ M(q)\ddot q + C(q,\dot q)\dot q + g(q) + f(\dot q) = \tau \]

The known first-principles model becomes a structural prior.

Physics residual

\[ r_{\mathrm{dyn}} = M(q)\ddot q_\theta + C(q,\dot q)\dot q + g(q) - \tau \]

Automatic differentiation can evaluate residuals during training.

Hybrid objective

\[ \mathcal L = \lambda_d \mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}} + \lambda_p \|r_{\mathrm{dyn}}\|^2 + \lambda_c \mathcal L_{\mathrm{constraint}} \]

Physics acts as an additional source of supervision.

The hybrid idea: a perfect analytical model is rarely available, but throwing physics away is equally wasteful. A robot's inertia, gravity, topology, geometry, constraints and conservation laws contain valuable information. The neural network should therefore learn model error, unknown interactions, or difficult nonlinear terms instead of rediscovering every physical principle from data.

Category 3

Quantum Computing for Robotics

Robotics contains computationally expensive problems: high-dimensional configuration search, combinatorial task planning, multi-robot allocation, nonconvex optimization and learning over large state-action spaces. Quantum computing introduces a fundamentally different computational model. The near-term opportunity is likely to be hybrid quantum-classical robotics : classical processors handle perception, robot control and physical interfaces, while quantum processors address selected optimization and learning subproblems.

Q

Quantum state

\[ |\psi\rangle = \sum_{i=0}^{2^n-1} \alpha_i |i\rangle \]

\(n\) qubits describe amplitudes over \(2^n\) computational basis states.

Variational quantum model

\[ |\psi(x,\theta)\rangle = U(\theta) U_{\phi}(x) |0\rangle^{\otimes n} \]

Classical robot states can be encoded into a parameterized quantum circuit.

Grover search

\[ O(N) \quad\rightarrow\quad O(\sqrt N) \]

For an unstructured search oracle, Grover's algorithm provides a quadratic query-complexity advantage.

Hassen Nigatu and collaborators, 2026. A broad review of quantum machine learning, quantum reinforcement learning, quantum optimization, path planning, multi-robot systems and opportunities for quantum-enhanced robotics.

DOI: 10.65218/jius.2026.13

Important scientific perspective: quantum robotics should not be presented as though today's quantum computers can simply replace classical robot computers. A professional analysis must separate theoretical quantum complexity advantages from end-to-end experimental speedup. Encoding cost, measurement cost, noise, circuit depth, oracle construction and strong classical baselines must all be considered.

The bigger picture

Where the three research directions meet

Mathematics provides structure

Screw theory, Lie groups, constraints and mechanism geometry describe what motions are physically possible.

Learning handles uncertainty

Physics-informed models learn friction, deformation, unknown dynamics and complex interactions that analytical models cannot capture perfectly.

Quantum computing targets complexity

Hybrid quantum algorithms may eventually accelerate selected optimization, learning and search problems that become expensive as robotic systems scale.

Long-term research vision: a future robot model may combine geometric mechanics, physics-informed learning and quantum optimization. The analytical model supplies symmetry, constraints and conservation structure; the neural model learns uncertainty and unmodeled physics; and a specialized quantum algorithm searches difficult configuration, planning or optimization spaces.
\[ \boxed{ \text{Geometric Robotics} + \text{Physics-Informed Learning} + \text{Quantum Optimization} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Hybrid Intelligent Robotics} } \]

Blog philosophy

Theory first, intelligence second, computation third.

The purpose of this blog is not simply to collect robotics formulas or report fashionable AI methods. The goal is to understand why a robot behaves the way it does, then identify which parts should be modeled analytically, which parts should be learned from data, and which computational bottlenecks may benefit from new computing paradigms.

The three categories therefore form a natural progression: mathematical robotics provides structure, physics-informed neural networks introduce adaptive intelligence without discarding structure, and quantum robotics investigates how future computation may address the most demanding search, optimization and learning problems.

\[ \text{Understand the geometry} \;\rightarrow\; \text{respect the physics} \;\rightarrow\; \text{learn the uncertainty} \;\rightarrow\; \text{optimize intelligently} \]
